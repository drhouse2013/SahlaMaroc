Marrying, registering a birth or getting a family book while living between two countries means juggling Moroccan law and the law of your country of residence. Mistakes cost months: an untranscribed record, an expired document, a forgotten apostille. This guide sets out the basics for an MRE or a mixed couple, separating what is well established from what you must have confirmed.

<Callout type="warning" title="Before anything else">
Requirements vary with **nationality**, **declared religion** and the **situation** of the future spouses, and they change. The lists below are the most common ones, not an exhaustive official list. **Confirm them** with the family court, the adoul or the consulate before building the file. This guide is not legal advice.
</Callout>

<Callout type="info" title="Law 55.19: fewer documents, but not everywhere (updated 17/10/2026)">
Law 55.19 on simplifying administrative procedures and formalities (dahir no. 1-20-06 of 6 March 2020) requires administrations to stop asking for certain documents and to accept a **sworn declaration** instead. The Interior Minister spoke of 22 documents removed, including celibacy and marriage certificates. The full official list is not public in the sources read, and a report relayed by the press in July 2025 notes that some administrations still ask for them. For a marriage, a consulate or a court may therefore demand more than the law: ask for the list in writing and keep a signed sworn declaration as a fallback.
</Callout>

## Basic civil-status documents

- **Birth certificate** (recent full copy): see [Moroccan birth certificate: full copy, Watiqa, consulate](/en/moroccan-birth-certificate-full-copy-extract-watiqa-mre);
- **Family book**, which gathers the family's records and is used in many procedures (travel with children, school, CNSS);
- **ID** (national ID card or passport, valid): see [renewing your Moroccan passport and ID abroad](/en/renew-moroccan-passport-id-abroad);
- **Proof of status**: divorce judgment or death certificate of a previous spouse if you were married before, sworn declaration or celibacy certificate depending on who asks.

Records issued abroad often need a **sworn translation** and an **apostille or legalisation**: see [apostille and legalisation](/en/apostille-legalisation-documents-morocco).

## Marrying in Morocco: steps

1. **Prepare the documents.** For each future spouse: full copy of the birth certificate, ID, medical certificate, administrative certificate or sworn declaration, photos. The file is kept at the family section's registry (art. 65 of the Family Code, per published summaries).
2. **File the authorisation request** with the family court where one of the spouses lives. The judge approves the file and authorises the adoul to draw up the record.
3. **Have the record drawn up by two adoul**, with the spouses, their witnesses and the marriage guardian if the law requires one. The marriage age is 18; marrying younger needs the judge's express authorisation (Family Code, art. 19 and 20).
4. **Collect the record and have the family book issued** by the civil registrar, with the marriage noted on the birth records.

Allow several weeks if documents are complete, longer if a judicial authorisation is needed: no single official timeframe was found.

## Mixed marriage: one Moroccan spouse, one foreign spouse

The file is heavier. The documents most often requested:

- **Certificate of capacity to marry** issued by the foreign spouse's embassy or consulate. Some nationalities are exempted by agreement: ask your embassy.
- **Criminal record extract** and a **medical certificate** issued in Morocco.
- **Certified passport copy**, with the entry stamp, and recent photos.
- **Documents on religious status**, because Moroccan law frames marriage by religion: a Muslim woman may not marry a non-Muslim man, and a Muslim man may marry a non-Muslim woman only if she belongs to the People of the Book (Family Code, art. 39, per published commentary). A non-Muslim spouse must be able to document their situation.
- **Judicial authorisation** for the foreigner's marriage.

Everything is generally required in several copies, translated and, for some records, apostilled.

## Marrying abroad as a Moroccan (MRE)

This is one of the most useful points for MRE. Under the Family Code (art. 14 and 15, as reported by the press and legal scholars; consult the official text), a Moroccan living abroad may conclude their marriage **under the administrative formalities of the country of residence** if the Moroccan substantive conditions are met: consent, capacity, no impediment, dower, and **two Muslim witnesses present**. A copy of the record must then be lodged **with the Moroccan consulate of the place of marriage within three months** according to these sources.

Two honest caveats. First, reform of the Family Code has been discussed since 2023, and a proposal to exempt MRE from the two Muslim witnesses had not been adopted according to 2026 press: check the current text on the Ministry of Justice portal. Second, recognition of a civil marriage by Moroccan courts is not automatic in a mixed context: it may depend on the judge seized.

## After the wedding: getting the record recognised

- **MRE family:** lodge the record at the consulate within the deadline so it is entered in the Moroccan civil registry and noted in the family book.
- **Country of residence:** a Moroccan record may need transcription to be recognised (in France, the central civil-status service handles foreign records of French nationals).
- **Birth of a child:** register it at the consulate. An older implementing decree of law 37-99 used 30 days; the press reports that a decree extended the transcription deadline to one year for MRE. Law 36-21 has since replaced law 37-99: confirm the current deadline with your consulate rather than waiting.

## Common mistakes

- **Trusting a document list found online** without having the court or consulate confirm it.
- **Apostilling too early or too late**: some records have a validity period (often three to six months).
- **Forgetting to transcribe** a marriage celebrated abroad: the family book then stays incomplete, with consequences for children and inheritance.
- **Spelling a name differently** across passport, record and ID: have it corrected before filing.
- **Skipping the sworn translation** and multiple copies.

## Checklist

<Checklist id="etat-civil" lang="en" title="Marriage and civil status: my checklist">

- Recent full copies of both spouses' birth certificates
- Proof of marital status (sworn declaration, divorce judgment or death certificate)
- Medical certificates
- Valid ID and certified copies
- Mixed marriage: certificate of capacity to marry and criminal record
- Documents translated and apostilled where needed
- Family judge's authorisation obtained if required
- Marriage abroad: record lodged at the consulate within the deadline
- Family book updated and copies kept

</Checklist>
